## Knowing words is not the same as understanding a text

Many learners assume that comprehension will come once their vocabulary is large enough. It helps, but many texts that feel hard are not hard because of unknown words. They are hard because the reader cannot see how the sentences fit together. A text can be full of familiar words and still leave you lost, because the meaning sits in the connections between them.

Comprehension is a set of habits. The strategies below are the ones that matter most for French, and each one can be practised on any text you already read.

## Predict before you read

Before reading a paragraph, look at its title, any photo, and the first sentence. Ask what the text is probably about, and what you expect to happen. Prediction does two things. It activates the vocabulary you already know for that topic, and it gives you a frame to check against as you read.

Try this with a short news headline. If the headline is *Grève des trains prévue jeudi*, you can guess the topic is a rail strike on Thursday before reading anything. The article then only has to confirm, add details and explain, which is much easier than starting from nothing.

## Read in meaning chunks

French sentences often run long. Rather than reading word by word, find the main verb and its subject first, then the object, then any clauses. Consider this sentence:

> *Bien que la pluie ait commencé tôt, les enfants sont sortis jouer dans le parc, parce qu'ils voulaient profiter de la journée.*

Read it in chunks:

- *Bien que la pluie ait commencé tôt*: although the rain started early (a concession)
- *les enfants sont sortis jouer dans le parc*: the children went out to play in the park (the main clause)
- *parce qu'ils voulaient profiter de la journée*: because they wanted to make the most of the day (the reason)

The main event is the children going out. Everything else adds context. Once you see that, the sentence is clear even though it is long.

## Track linking words

Linking words carry much of the meaning in French. *Pourtant*, *cependant*, *donc*, *alors*, *bien que* and *parce que* tell you how one idea relates to the next. When you meet one, pause and ask what relationship it signals: contrast, cause, consequence, or concession. Missing these is one of the most common reasons a text that seems clear turns out to have been misunderstood.

## Use context to resolve unknown words

When a word is unknown, read the whole sentence and then the next one before deciding what it means. The surrounding words usually narrow the meaning down. A word that is an action in one sentence may name an object in another, and the context tells you which.

Check a dictionary only when the meaning still matters after you have tried this. Otherwise you can often carry on, which keeps your reading moving and makes the word easier to remember later.

## Identify who is doing what

French uses pronouns heavily. *Il*, *elle*, *lui*, *le*, *en*, *y* and *qui* all refer back to earlier nouns. Comprehension slips when you lose track of who a pronoun stands for. When a pronoun appears, glance back to find its noun. This habit matters most in longer texts with several people, where a mistake can change the whole story.

## Summarise in French

After a paragraph, summarise its main point in one short French sentence, without copying phrases. If you cannot do it, the paragraph has a gap: a word, a link, or a reference you missed. Go back to find it. Summarising is a reliable test because it requires you to build meaning, not just recognise it.

## A practice routine

1. **Predict.** Read the title and first sentence and guess the topic in a few words.
2. **Read in chunks.** Find the main verb and subject in each sentence.
3. **Mark linking words.** Note whether each one signals contrast, cause, consequence or concession.
4. **Resolve pronouns.** When a pronoun appears, identify its noun.
5. **Summarise.** Write one French sentence per paragraph.
6. **Check.** Look back at the text only to confirm your summary, then note any gap.

Do this with one short passage a day, or three or four times a week for a longer text. Keep a record of how many paragraphs you could summarise without looking back. That number is a useful measure of progress.

## Choose text that makes practice possible

These strategies work best on text where you know most of the words. If you are checking every sentence, the problem is the level of the text, not your method.
