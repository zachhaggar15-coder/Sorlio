## Spelling is not random

French spelling looks chaotic to English eyes. Silent letters, odd combinations and nasal sounds seem designed to confuse. But French is more regular than it looks. A set of rules maps spelling to sound consistently, and once you know them you can read most words aloud with reasonable accuracy, even ones you have never seen.

This guide covers the patterns that help most. Pronunciation approximations here are rough and use English as a reference, so treat them as a starting point and listen to a native speaker whenever you can.

## Silent final letters

Most final consonants are silent. The common ones are *s*, *t*, *d*, *x*, *z*, *p* and *g*.

> *petit*, *grand*, *vous*, *trop*, *beaucoup*, *deux*, *nez*

There is a useful exception. Final **c, r, f and l** are often pronounced. A memory aid is the word **CaReFuL**.

> *avec*, *bonjour*, *neuf*, *mal*, *sac*, *hiver*

It is not perfect (*les clés* has a silent *s*, and *parler* ends in a silent *r*), but it covers a lot of ground.

## The vowels that look familiar but are not

| Spelling | Sound (approximate) | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *ou* | like "oo" in food | *vous*, *nous*, *pour* |
| *u* | say "ee" with rounded lips | *tu*, *rue*, *du* |
| *oi* | like "wah" | *moi*, *toi*, *voir* |
| *eau*, *au* | like "oh" | *beau*, *chaud*, *bateau* |
| *ai*, *ei* | like "eh" | *mais*, *français*, *neige* |
| *eu*, *œu* | rounded "uh" | *peur*, *heure*, *œuf* |

The French *u* is a real stumbling block for English speakers. Try saying "ee" and then round your lips as if to say "oo", keeping your tongue where it is.

## Nasal vowels

French has four nasal vowels, where air passes through the nose and the *n* itself is not pronounced.

| Spelling | Example | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *an*, *en* | *enfant*, *dans*, *vent* | Open, like "ahn" without the n |
| *on* | *bon*, *nom*, *maison* | Rounded, like "ohn" without the n |
| *in*, *ain*, *ein* | *vin*, *pain*, *plein* | Like "ahn" through the nose, lighter |
| *un* | *un*, *lundi* | Closely related to *in*; many speakers merge them |

If an *n* is followed by a vowel, it is not nasal: *bon* (nasal), *bonne* (not nasal).

## Consonant groups to know

| Spelling | Sound | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *ch* | "sh" | *chat*, *chambre*, *machine* |
| *gn* | like "ny" in canyon | *montagne*, *signe*, *ligne* |
| *ç* | "s" | *français*, *garçon*, *leçon* |
| *qu* | "k" | *que*, *qui*, *quatre* |
| *ph* | "f" | *photo*, *pharmacie* |
| *h* | silent | *homme*, *hôtel*, *heure* |

The French *r* is made at the back of the throat, not with the tongue tip. It takes practice, and listening is the best teacher.

## Liaison

Liaison is a silent final consonant that comes back when the next word starts with a vowel.

> *les amis* → "lay-zah-mee"
> *vous avez* → "voo-zah-vay"
> *un petit enfant* → the *t* of *petit* is pronounced before *enfant*

Liaison is mandatory in some places (after *les*, *des*, *un* and before a noun), forbidden in others, and optional in many. As a reader, you only need to know that a silent letter can wake up before a vowel.

## A reading routine

1. **Choose a short paragraph** you already understand.
2. **Mark silent letters** at the ends of words, remembering CaReFuL.
3. **Read it aloud slowly,** applying the sound patterns.
4. **Listen to a native recording** of the same text and compare.
5. **Record yourself** and listen back.

Ten minutes a few times a week makes a visible difference. Reading aloud also helps meanings attach to French words, which supports comprehension.

## Practice

Say these aloud, then check against the patterns above.

1. *beaucoup* (silent *p*)
2. *maison* (nasal *on*)
3. *chambre* (*ch* is "sh", nasal *am*)
4. *montagne* (*gn* is "ny")
5. *les amis* (liaison)
