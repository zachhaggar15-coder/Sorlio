## Why translating feels necessary

Most learners start by translating. You read a sentence, convert each word into English, and put the English back together. At the start this is the only way to get any meaning at all. The problem is that it becomes a habit long after it stops helping. Translation is slow, it piles up at longer sentences, and it leaves you reading in English while you pretend to read French.

The aim is not to stop understanding through English altogether. It is to reach a point where a French sentence produces meaning without an English sentence forming in your head first. This takes practice, and it is easier to build deliberately than to wait for.

## What word-by-word translation costs you

Translating word by word has three hidden costs:

- **Speed.** Each word waits for its English equivalent, so reading slows to the pace of your translation rather than your comprehension.
- **Word order.** French often places adjectives, pronouns and objects in different positions from English. Translating in order can produce an English sentence that means something different.
- **Memory.** Words learned as English equivalents tend to stay attached to English. You recognise *maison* as "house", but you may not hear *maison* as a place you live.

Vocabulary research suggests that words are learned more durably when you meet them repeatedly in running text, where meaning comes from context, than when you learn them only as translations (Nation, 2001). Context does some of the work that a dictionary otherwise does.

## Step 1: Choose text you mostly understand

Translation is a symptom of text that is too hard. If you have to translate most sentences, the answer is not a better method but easier material. Pick a text where you understand the general meaning of most paragraphs and can name the main subject. Look up only the few words that block the sense.

Krashen's idea that useful input sits just beyond what you already know is relevant here (Krashen, 1985). Text far above your level will push you back into translation.

## Step 2: Read in meaning chunks

Instead of translating word by word, read in chunks: the subject and verb, then the object, then the place or time. Many French sentences are built from predictable blocks.

> *Hier, Marc a pris le train pour Lyon, parce qu'il avait rendez-vous avec sa sœur.*

Read it in chunks rather than in English order:

- *Hier*: yesterday, a time marker at the front.
- *Marc a pris le train*: Marc took the train (*a pris* is the past of *prendre*).
- *pour Lyon*: to Lyon.
- *parce qu'il avait rendez-vous avec sa sœur*: because he had an appointment with his sister.

You do not need a word-for-word English version. The meaning is already there once you have the chunks.

## Step 3: Let context settle unknown words

When you meet an unknown word, read the whole sentence before deciding what it means. Consider this:

> *Le médecin a dit que le patient devait se reposer pendant une semaine.*

If you do not know *reposer*, the context tells you a lot: a doctor, a patient, a week, and rest. *Se reposer* means "to rest". You can understand the sentence without a dictionary, and the verb will be easier to remember because you met it in a meaningful situation.

Check the word only if the sentence still does not make sense, or if the word keeps appearing and clearly matters.

## Step 4: Practise the direct route

Direct reading is a skill, so practise it on purpose:

1. **Read a paragraph once** without stopping for any word. Note the sense in one line of French or English.
2. **Read it again** and look up only the words that blocked you.
3. **Read it aloud** at a speed you can manage. Hearing the sentence in French helps the meaning attach to the French words.
4. **Summarise in French** in one or two short sentences. A summary forces you to build meaning directly.

A short daily session of this is worth more than a long session of translation, because it trains the path you want to use. That is a practical judgement, not a measured result, so notice how it works for you.

## Step 5: Watch for the moment it starts to work

You will notice the change gradually. Some signs:

- You understand the gist of a paragraph before you finish reading it.
- You can answer a question about a passage without reconstructing the English.
- You stop translating idioms and understand them as wholes.

Those signs suggest the direct route is becoming your default. It is normal for translation to come back when you hit something difficult. That is a sign to choose easier material, not a sign that you have failed.

## A short practice routine

- **Five minutes:** read a short passage once for meaning, with no stops.
- **Five minutes:** reread it and look up three words at most.
- **Three minutes:** write a one-sentence summary in French.
- **Once a week:** choose a slightly harder text, and check how many sentences you still translate.

Keep a short note of how many sentences you needed to translate in a given session. Over a few weeks the number should fall.
