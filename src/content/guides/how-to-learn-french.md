## Start with a goal you can check

Most people who stop learning French stop because the goal was vague. "Get good at French" has no finish line, so it is easy to drift. A better goal is specific and checkable: read a short news article without a dictionary for most of it, hold a five-minute conversation about your job, or pass an A2 exam in nine months.

Write your goal down and be honest about the time you have. Ten minutes a day for six months is a realistic plan for many people. An hour a day for a month is less likely to last. Choose the plan you will actually keep.

## Know what the levels mean

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) describes six levels, from A1 to C2. A broad summary:

- **A1 and A2 (basic user):** you can understand familiar, everyday phrases and simple texts, and handle basic exchanges.
- **B1 and B2 (independent user):** you can follow the main points of clear speech and articles on familiar topics, and talk through most everyday situations.
- **C1 and C2 (proficient user):** you can understand demanding texts and speak fluently and precisely.

The levels are a map, not a test you pass by memorising the descriptions. Use them to decide what material to read and what to practise next. The CEFR descriptors are published by the Council of Europe, and they are a useful common reference when you compare resources.

## Vocabulary: the foundation

You cannot read or speak without words, so vocabulary deserves steady attention. The most useful words are high-frequency ones: common verbs such as *être*, *avoir*, *aller* and *faire*, and everyday nouns and adjectives. Research on vocabulary and reading suggests that learners need to know a high share of the words in a text to understand it well (Hu and Nation, 2000; Nation, 2001).

Two practical habits help most:

1. **Learn words in phrases, not alone.** *Prendre le train* is easier to remember and use than *prendre* by itself.
2. **Review at spaced intervals.** A word you revisit after a day, then after a week, sticks better than one you cram once.

## Grammar: understand the patterns

Grammar is the set of patterns that lets you combine words. You do not need to memorise every rule on day one. Focus on the structures that appear most often in the texts you read:

- the present tense of the most common verbs
- the *passé composé* for completed actions
- the near future, *aller* plus an infinitive, for plans
- gender and articles, *le*, *la*, *les*, *un*, *une*
- basic word order, including where pronouns sit in the sentence

When you meet a pattern in a text, look at two or three more examples and try to write your own sentence with it. That is more effective than rereading the rule.

## Reading comprehension

Reading is the most efficient way to build vocabulary and grammar together, provided the text is not too hard. Choose material where you understand most of the words, and use the unfamiliar ones as a learning opportunity. Our [guide to learning French through reading](/learn-french-by-reading/) explains this approach in detail.

## Listening practice

Listening is harder than reading because you cannot pause a conversation to check a word. Start with material designed for learners, such as slow news bulletins or short audio stories, and listen to the same text several times. Gradually move to faster and less scripted audio. Shadowing, where you repeat a sentence aloud just after you hear it, is a useful bridge between listening and speaking.

## Speaking and pronunciation

Speaking is a separate skill, and it needs its own practice. Even short sessions help. Read a paragraph aloud, record yourself, and compare it with a native recording. Look for a conversation partner, a tutor or a language exchange once you have a basic vocabulary, because you cannot learn to talk by reading alone.

## Writing

Writing consolidates grammar. Keep a notebook of short paragraphs about your day, then correct them against a reference. Writing a few sentences a week gives you a clear picture of which structures you still struggle with.

## Consistency beats intensity

A steady routine matters more than a heavy one. Plan a fixed time, keep sessions short, and track what you did. If you miss a day, return the next day rather than trying to catch up in one long session. Over months, a modest routine adds up to a great deal of French.

## Choosing resources

Pick a small set of resources and use them in a pattern:

- one **structured course or grammar reference** for explanations
- one **reading source** matched to your level
- one **listening source** that you can repeat
- one **speaking practice**, even if it is occasional

Avoid collecting apps. Every additional tool adds setup and reduces the time you spend on French itself.

## Sample study plans

These are starting points. Adjust them to your level and schedule.

### Ten minutes a day (complete beginner)

| Day | Activity | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mon to Fri | Learn 8 new words in phrases; review yesterday's words | 10 min |
| Saturday | Listen to a short, slow beginner audio and repeat three sentences aloud | 10 min |
| Sunday | Rest, or write three sentences about your week | Optional |

### Thirty minutes a day (A1 to A2)

| Day | Activity | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mon, Wed, Fri | Grammar: one structure from a reference, with five examples | 15 min |
| Mon, Wed, Fri | Reading: a short graded text, with words looked up as needed | 15 min |
| Tue, Thu | Vocabulary review and a short spoken paragraph, recorded | 30 min |
| Weekend | Longer reading or listening, with a summary written in French | 40 min |

### An hour a day (working towards B1)

| Day | Activity | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily | Vocabulary review with spaced intervals | 10 min |
| Mon, Wed, Fri | Grammar and exercises from a course | 20 min |
| Tue, Thu | Reading a news or story text at your level | 20 min |
| Daily or most days | Listening to podcast or news audio, then a short summary | 15 min |
| Weekend | Speaking with a partner or tutor, plus writing a short paragraph | 45 min |

## Your first week

Do these five things before you plan anything else:

1. **Write your goal** in one sentence, with a level and a date.
2. **Pick your three resources:** one for explanations, one for reading, one for listening.
3. **Block a fixed time** each day, even ten minutes, and put it in your calendar.
4. **Do a level check** on a short text (see our [reading level guide](/check-your-french-reading-level/)) so you start at the right difficulty.
5. **Review on day seven.** Note what you finished and what you skipped, and shrink the plan if it was too big.
