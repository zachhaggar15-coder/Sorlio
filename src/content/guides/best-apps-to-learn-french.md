## There is no single best app

Searching for the best app to learn French usually returns a single ranking. That is convenient, but it hides something important: the right app depends on what you need. A beginner who has never studied a language needs structure and short lessons. An intermediate reader who wants to finish a novel needs texts and a way to save words. A person preparing for a speaking exam needs conversation. Each of those needs points to a different tool.

This guide groups apps by need, so you can work out which category you are in and then choose from there. We are the publishers of Sorlio, which appears in one category below. We have tried to describe the others fairly, and we say where another type of app is the better choice.

## Start with the question you are trying to answer

Before choosing an app, answer three questions:

1. **What is your current level?** Roughly, can you read a simple sentence, hold a basic conversation, or neither? The [CEFR framework](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages) describes levels from A1 (beginner) to C2 (proficient), and it is a useful common reference.
2. **What do you want to do with French in six months?** Read a book, pass a test, speak with colleagues, or simply enjoy a trip?
3. **How much time can you give it each day?** Ten focused minutes is a different plan from an hour at the weekend.

If you can answer these, the categories below will help you narrow the field quickly.

## Absolute beginners

**Best fit:** people starting from zero who want a guided path, a daily habit and very short lessons.

Structured beginner apps introduce vocabulary, sentence patterns and basic grammar in a fixed order. They are good at building a habit and at giving you the first few hundred words. Their limitation is that they tend to keep you in short exercises for longer than you need, and they rarely expose you to natural French at length. Use them as a starting point, and add real reading as soon as you can manage it.

Duolingo is the best-known example of this kind of app. Its strengths are the habit-building structure and a free entry point. If you want more on how it compares with reading-first tools, see our [Duolingo alternatives guide](/duolingo-alternatives-for-french/).

## Structured study

**Best fit:** learners who want an ordered syllabus, explanations of grammar and a clear sense of progress through the levels.

If you like textbooks and want to know exactly what comes next, look for a course that is tied to CEFR levels and explains grammar in English. A course is usually slower than an app but more thorough. Many learners combine one structured course with a reading or listening habit, which is a more reliable pattern than relying on a single tool.

## Reading comprehension

**Best fit:** people who can already handle basic French and want to read stories, news or books.

Reading practice builds comprehension in a way that exercises do not. The key skill is working out meaning from context while knowing enough words to keep going. Apps built around reading typically offer graded stories, imported articles or both. Our [reading-apps comparison](/best-french-reading-apps/) looks at this group in detail.

Sorlio belongs in this category. It is an Android app for reading French stories and news, with tap-to-look-up vocabulary, saved words and listening while reading. It is most useful once you can read simple text, because the reading itself is the main activity. Check the Google Play listing for which content and features are available on your plan.

## Vocabulary acquisition

**Best fit:** people who already read a little and want an efficient way to remember words.

Flashcard-style tools are strong at spaced repetition, which means reviewing a word just before you are likely to forget it. They work well for building a large vocabulary quickly. Their weakness is that a word on its own is easy to forget, so pair them with reading, where the same words appear in sentences you care about. A vocabulary tool that you use with your own reading gives you the context that a flashcard lacks.

## Conversation and pronunciation

**Best fit:** people whose main goal is speaking.

No reading or grammar app will make you a confident speaker on its own. Look for tools with speech recognition, live tutors or partner matching, and plan regular speaking sessions even if they are short. For many learners the most effective combination is a reading habit for input and a weekly conversation for output.

Sorlio is not a speaking tool. If speaking is your priority, choose something built for it and use reading as a supplement.

## Grammar practice

**Best fit:** learners who find grammar confusing and want to practise specific structures.

Grammar is easier to learn when it is shown in use. Good grammar practice explains a rule, gives examples and then asks you to apply it. Check whether an app shows you why an answer is right, not just whether it is right. A grammar reference book or a course with explanations often works better for this than a gamified exercise.

## Authentic content

**Best fit:** intermediate and advanced learners who want to read and listen to real French.

Authentic content means the material native speakers read or hear: news, podcasts, novels and video. The advantage is relevance and variety. The disadvantage is difficulty. Start with content slightly below your level, and use a tool that makes unfamiliar words easy to look up.

## How to decide

- **Complete beginner, want a habit:** a structured beginner app, plus a weekly look at very simple reading.
- **Want a syllabus and grammar explanations:** a structured course.
- **Can read a little, want to read more:** a reading app, such as Sorlio, LingQ or Readlang.
- **Want to speak:** a speaking tool or tutor, with reading as support.
- **Want a large vocabulary:** a spaced-repetition tool that you combine with reading.

Most learners end up using two or three of these. Choose the one that matches your current gap, rather than the one with the most features.
