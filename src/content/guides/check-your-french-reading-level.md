## Why most learners pick texts the wrong way

Many learners choose reading material by label. A book marked "for beginners" is assumed to be right, and a news site is assumed to be too hard. Neither assumption is reliable. Labels vary between publishers, and a text's real difficulty depends on its sentence length, its vocabulary and how much of it you can predict.

A better method is to test yourself on short samples before committing to a whole text. This takes five minutes and saves a lot of frustration. It is also a useful way to check whether a text is worth your time.

## The three signals to watch

When you read a sample, notice three things:

1. **Coverage.** Roughly how many words do you know on sight? Research on reading in a second language has linked comprehension to the share of known words in a text, with a high share needed for comfortable reading (Hu and Nation, 2000). You do not need a precise figure. Count the unknown words in one paragraph.
2. **Sentence load.** Are sentences short enough to hold in mind, or do they run over several clauses with subordinate pronouns and linked ideas?
3. **Predictability.** Can you guess the topic from the first line? Familiar topics make unknown words easier to infer.

A text is comfortable when you know most words, the sentences are manageable, and the topic is predictable. It is too hard when a single paragraph forces you to look up many words before you can follow it.

## A five-step self-test

Work through these in order and stop when a passage stops being comfortable.

**Step 1: Read a level A1 sample.** Sample: *Je m'appelle Karim. J'habite à Lyon avec ma sœur. Le matin, je prends le bus pour aller au travail. J'aime le café et le pain.* If you understand all of this without looking anything up, move on. If not, begin with A1 material.

**Step 2: Read a level A2 sample.** Sample: *La semaine dernière, Léa a visité un musée avec ses amis. Ils ont vu beaucoup de tableaux, mais ils étaient fatigués. Ensuite, ils ont mangé dans un petit restaurant près du fleuve.* You should follow the sequence of events. The *passé composé* (*ont vu*, *ont mangé*) is the main thing to check. If you can retell this in your own words, A2 reading is within reach.

**Step 3: Read a level B1 sample.** Sample: *Bien que le projet ait été retardé, l'équipe a décidé de continuer, car elle croyait que les résultats justifieraient les efforts supplémentaires.* This needs the subjunctive (*ait été*), a concessive link (*bien que*) and a reason clause. If you can say the main point, you are ready for B1 texts. If you need to unpick the structure, practise B1 grammar before long texts.

**Step 4: Time one paragraph.** Read a real article paragraph for meaning. Note how many words you looked up. If it is more than three or four per paragraph, drop to an easier level.

**Step 5: Test with a question.** After reading, answer three questions in English: what happened, who was involved, and why it matters. Being able to answer confirms that you understood, rather than that you merely recognised words.

## Score yourself

After each sample, rate how well you followed it:

| Result | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Followed it all without lookups | The level is comfortable | Move on to the next sample |
| Followed the main point, needed one or two lookups | The level is right for learning | Read at this level for a few weeks |
| Followed only parts of it | The level is too hard for now | Drop back one level and build vocabulary |
| Could not follow it | The level is well above you | Start two levels lower |

The level where you land in the second or third row is where most of your reading should be.

## Turning the result into a choice

- **Stumbling on A1 sample:** focus on basic verbs and high-frequency words first.
- **Comfortable with A2, stumbling on B1:** choose A2-level stories or simple news, and read them several times a week.
- **Comfortable with B1 sample, struggling with real paragraphs:** the problem is probably length and vocabulary breadth, not grammar. Read longer texts on topics you know.

Check your result again after a month. Reading level changes faster than most learners expect, and a text that was hard in September can be easy by November.

## A common mistake

Learners often judge difficulty by the first line of a text, which can be simple, and then get stuck halfway. Sample from the middle of a text, not only the opening. Also avoid judging by a single word you do not know. One unfamiliar noun rarely makes a passage too hard. A dense paragraph full of unfamiliar verbs and connectors usually does.
