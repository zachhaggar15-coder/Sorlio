## Why a novel is worth the effort

A novel gives you something short texts cannot: hundreds of pages of the same voice, the same characters and the same vocabulary. Words you struggle with in chapter one become familiar by chapter five. That repetition is what builds reading fluency. Research on extensive reading has long linked reading large amounts of accessible text with gains in reading ability (Day and Bamford, 1998).

A novel is also a goal, and a finished book is a concrete sign of progress that exercises cannot give.

## Step 1: Choose a book you can finish

The most common mistake is choosing a book that is too hard because it is famous. A classic that is a masterpiece in French can be a slog for a learner. Choose by readability, not reputation.

Look for:

- **Short chapters and short sentences.** These make the book feel manageable.
- **Dialogue.** Conversation is easier to follow than long descriptions.
- **A plot that pulls you forward.** Mystery, adventure and contemporary fiction work well.
- **A topic you actually enjoy.** Motivation matters more than the level label.

Detective novels with plain prose are commonly recommended for learners, and short contemporary fiction is another good route. A well-known children's classic can be a gentle first book, though its language is sometimes more subtle than it looks, so test a page first.

## Step 2: Do the five-page test

Before you commit, read one page from the middle of the book and count the words you do not know.

- **Zero to three unknown words:** this is easy. You can read it for pleasure.
- **Four to ten:** this is a good learning level. Expect to look up some words.
- **More than about ten:** this is probably too hard right now. Choose another book, or return to it later.

Research on reading comprehension suggests that a high density of unknown words makes a text hard to follow (Hu and Nation, 2000). The five-page test is a practical way to check that density for yourself.

## Step 3: Set a daily target you can keep

A novel is read page by page. Decide how much you will read each day, and make it small.

| Pace | What it looks like | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gentle | Five pages a day | A short novel in a month or two |
| Steady | Ten pages a day | A short novel in three to six weeks |
| Intensive | Twenty pages a day | Only if the book is easy for you |

Choose the pace you will actually keep. A book read at five pages a day is finished. A book abandoned after a burst of enthusiasm is not.

## Step 4: Decide your lookup rule

Before you open the book, set a rule for unknown words. A good one:

1. **Do not stop** on a word on the first appearance, unless the sentence makes no sense without it.
2. **Underline** it if you meet it again within the same chapter.
3. **Look it up** the second or third time.
4. **Keep a short list** of words that return often.

Names, places and invented words never need a dictionary. If a word is a character's name, move on.

## Step 5: Use help without losing the thread

You can combine reading with audio. Listening to a chapter before or after reading it helps your ear and your reading speed. If you find a long paragraph difficult, read it once for meaning, then reread it. A summary of the chapter in your own words, written or spoken, is the best check that you followed it.

## A six-week plan

| Week | Focus | Target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Settle in: test the book, learn the main characters | Ten to fifteen pages a day, few lookups |
| 2 | Find your rhythm: set your lookup rule | Same pace |
| 3 | Push through the slow middle | Stay on target, skip passages that drag |
| 4 | Review: look back at words that kept returning | Reread a short chapter |
| 5 | Momentum: the plot usually speeds up | Read a little more if you can |
| 6 | Finish: read the ending without stopping | Write a short summary of the book |

## When to give up

It is fine to stop. If after fifty pages the book is still a struggle and you dread opening it, it is the wrong book, not the wrong learner. Swap it for something easier. Reading a book you enjoy teaches more than finishing one you resent.
