## What we mean by a reading app

A reading app is a tool built around text you read in French, rather than around short exercises. Some apps give you graded stories written for learners. Others let you import an article, a book excerpt or a subtitle file and work through it yourself. Most add a dictionary, a way to save words, and some kind of review.

The useful question is not "which app is best?" It is "which app will make me read more French, and understand more of it, in the time I actually have?" This guide compares four options so you can answer that for yourself. It is written by the team behind Sorlio, so read it with that in mind. We have tried to describe competitors fairly and to say where they are the better choice.

## The short answer

- **If you want graded, ready-made reading with a clear path,** a graded-reader approach or a dedicated reading course is often a good fit.
- **If you want to import your own texts and build a large saved vocabulary,** a tool built around imported content, such as LingQ or Readlang, is worth a trial.
- **If you want side-by-side reading and listening for beginners,** an app that pairs translated or parallel text with audio may suit you best.
- **If you want French stories and news on Android with tap-to-look-up vocabulary, saved words and review,** Sorlio is designed for that workflow. It is an Android app only.

## Where each one is strongest

**LingQ** is built around the idea that you learn by reading and listening to material you choose, and saving the words you do not know. Its strength is the large amount of content and the steady building of a personal vocabulary. The trade-off is that it asks for a bit of setup and self-discipline, and the content is only as easy as what you pick.

**Readlang** suits people who already have their own reading. If you read French news sites or a novel you have downloaded, it lets you look up words in place and turn them into review cards. It is less of a structured learning path than a reading tool.

**Beelinguapp** is well suited to someone who is just starting with stories. Parallel text and audio let you follow the story without knowing every word, which is a useful way to build confidence early on.

**Sorlio** is a good fit for a learner who wants French reading practice on an Android phone that combines graded stories and news, tap-to-look-up vocabulary, saved words and listening. Its particular focus is learning vocabulary in the context of a sentence you are actually reading, rather than in isolation.

## Choosing for your situation

**You are a complete beginner.** Start with a course or graded material that explains basic grammar. A reading app will be frustrating until you know common verbs and sentence patterns. Beelinguapp-style parallel reading can help you bridge that early gap.

**You can read simple French but get stuck on most paragraphs.** This is where a tap-to-look-up approach is most useful. Choose content slightly below your level, where you know about 95 to 98 percent of the words, and look up the rest as you go. Research on reading comprehension suggests that a small number of unknown words per page is manageable, while a high density of them makes reading hard to follow (Hu and Nation, 2000).

**You are intermediate and want to read real French.** Imported articles, novels and news are the next step. LingQ and Readlang are designed for this. Sorlio's news and stories are also useful here if you prefer an app-based routine on Android.

**You need speaking or live conversation.** None of these reading apps replaces speaking practice. Pair reading with a conversation partner, a tutor or a speaking-focused tool.

## Limitations to weigh

- None of these apps guarantees a level or an outcome. Reading builds vocabulary and comprehension, but grammar, pronunciation and speaking need their own practice.
- Free access and paid plans differ between products, and features move around. Verify current details on the product's site or store listing.
- The feature descriptions in this article reflect what we could verify from public sources at the time of writing. Treat them as a starting point.

## Sources and method

This comparison describes approach and fit, not measured results. We did not run controlled tests of learning outcomes, and we have not published any ranking based on user data. If you want to compare the apps yourself, a good test is to spend a week with each one on the same kind of text and note how often you need to look things up and how much you remember the next day.
