## Why French words slip away

Most learners do not forget words because they are hard. They forget them because they met them once, in isolation, and never needed them again. A list of twenty words read on a Sunday is almost gone by Wednesday. The brain keeps what it uses, in situations that matter, at intervals that are spaced far enough apart that recall takes some effort.

That gives you the three working principles for this guide: learn words in sentences, review them at spaced intervals, and practise recalling them rather than recognising them.

## Learn words in sentences, not lists

A word on its own gives you a meaning. A word in a sentence gives you a meaning plus the grammar around it: whether it takes a preposition, whether it is a noun with an article, and what kind of situation it describes.

Take *chercher*. On a list it is "to look for". In a sentence it becomes useful:

> *Je cherche mes clés depuis dix minutes.*
> I have been looking for my keys for ten minutes.

Now you have the verb, the present tense used for an ongoing action with *depuis*, and an everyday situation you can picture. When you save a word, save the sentence it came from. That sentence is your best memory cue.

## Use spaced review

Spaced review means revisiting a word just before you would forget it. A simple schedule works well: review a new word the next day, then after three days, then after a week, then after a few weeks. Each successful recall makes the next gap longer. If you forget a word, bring it back sooner.

The research on second-language vocabulary consistently finds that repeated meetings, spread over time, help words move from short-term recognition to lasting knowledge (Nation, 2001). You do not need a special app to do this. A notebook with dates works, though software can handle the schedule for you.

## Practise recall, not recognition

Reading a word and nodding is recognition. Covering the English, saying the French from memory, and then checking is recall. Recall is harder, and it is the part that builds lasting memory. Use it in three ways:

1. **Cover and recall.** Look at the French with the English hidden, and say the meaning aloud before checking.
2. **Produce a sentence.** Write one new sentence using the word, even a simple one.
3. **Retell.** After reading a short passage, summarise it in French without looking back, and notice which words you reached for.

## Keep the list small and useful

A saved list of 500 words that you never review is less useful than 50 words you know well. Keep your list focused:

- save a word when it appears at least twice, or when it clearly matters to you
- save the word with the sentence it came from
- remove words you have recalled correctly many times, so the list stays manageable
- review a fixed number each day, so review never becomes an overwhelming backlog

Common high-frequency words, such as *aller*, *faire*, *prendre* and *avoir l'air*, deserve early attention, because they appear in almost every text you read.

## Use reading to review

Reading is the best review you can do, because it shows the same words in new sentences and forms. A word you learned as *chercher* appears as *cherché*, *cherchais*, *cherchons*, and you start to recognise the root. Read a passage at a level where you know most of the words, and notice how many saved words come back. Each return is a spaced meeting you did not have to schedule.

## A weekly routine

- **Daily, five to ten minutes:** review words due today, and add up to five new words from your reading, each with its sentence.
- **Three times a week:** read a short passage, look up only the words that block meaning, and save those that recur.
- **Once a week:** check which saved words you can recall without looking, and remove the ones you have mastered.

Keep the routine short enough to sustain. Consistency over months matters far more than a single intense session.
