## How to use context without guessing wildly

A sentence can narrow the meaning of an unfamiliar word without revealing an exact translation. Look for the situation, grammatical role and nearby contrast; make a provisional guess, then check it. The original examples below are exercises, not a substitute for a dictionary. Cover the answers while you try them.

## Exercise 1: use the situation

*Avant de sortir sous la pluie, Nadia prend son parapluie.*

What object is Nadia likely taking? Which words help you decide? Is the object a verb or a noun?

**Answer:** An umbrella. *Sous la pluie* means “in the rain”, and *prend son* indicates that she takes an object. The situation makes umbrella plausible, but looking up *parapluie* confirms it. A situation alone can mislead: she might also take a coat in the rain.

## Exercise 2: use contrast

*Le café est ouvert le matin, mais il est fermé le dimanche.*

What does *fermé* likely mean? What does *mais* signal?

**Answer:** Closed. *Mais* introduces a contrast with *ouvert* (open). The sentence says it is open in the morning but closed on Sunday; it does not specify opening times for every other day.

## Exercise 3: use a result

*Hugo a oublié ses clés ; il ne peut pas ouvrir la porte.*

What did Hugo forget? Which result points to your answer?

**Answer:** His keys. He cannot open the door. The plural possessive *ses* suggests a plural noun; it does not by itself tell you what the objects are.

## Exercise 4: a guess needs checking

*Elle cherche une place dans le train, mais tous les sièges sont occupés.*

Does *place* mean a town square here? What is she looking for?

**Answer:** A seat or space on the train. The words *train* and *sièges* (seats) narrow the meaning. *Place* has several meanings, so a familiar English-looking word is not always enough to choose the right one. *Occupés* means occupied: all the seats are taken.

## A repeatable reading routine

1. Read the sentence and the one before it. Identify who is doing what and where.
2. Decide whether the unknown item is a noun, verb or modifier. Articles and verb endings can provide clues, though you may need grammar help.
3. Substitute a broad idea such as “an object” or “a movement”. Can you still follow the passage?
4. Check words that are central, recur, or change the conclusion. A dictionary can distinguish plausible alternatives.
5. Save a useful word with its original sentence and revisit it later. Do not assume a single translation fits every context.

Consider *fermé* again. If a notice says *fermé sauf le dimanche*, the exception *sauf* reverses a quick assumption. Small words such as *ne…pas*, *mais* and *sauf* can matter more than an unfamiliar noun. Read carefully when the text concerns a real appointment, instruction or safety decision.
