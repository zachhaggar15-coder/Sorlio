## Negation has two parts

Most French negation wraps the verb. A small word, *ne*, goes before it, and a second word goes after. The second word decides what kind of negative it is. English uses a single word, such as "not" or "never", so the wrapped structure takes a moment to get used to.

The key habit for readers: when you see *ne* or *n'*, hold your attention for the second word. That word tells you the meaning.

## The common pairs

| Pair | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *ne… pas* | not | *Il ne parle pas.* (He does not speak.) |
| *ne… jamais* | never | *Elle ne ment jamais.* (She never lies.) |
| *ne… plus* | no longer, not any more | *Je ne fume plus.* (I don't smoke any more.) |
| *ne… rien* | nothing | *Il n'a rien dit.* (He said nothing.) |
| *ne… personne* | nobody, no one | *Je ne connais personne ici.* (I know nobody here.) |
| *ne… pas encore* | not yet | *Ils ne sont pas encore arrivés.* (They have not arrived yet.) |
| *ne… aucun* | no, not one | *Il n'y a aucun problème.* (There is no problem.) |
| *ne… que* | only | *Il n'a que deux euros.* (He has only two euros.) |

## Watch the compound tenses

In the passé composé, the negative wraps the auxiliary, not the participle:

> *Je n'ai pas mangé.* (I have not eaten.)
> *Elle n'a jamais vu la mer.* (She has never seen the sea.)

There is one wrinkle. *Rien* stays before the participle, but *personne* goes after it:

> *Je n'ai rien vu.* (I saw nothing.)
> *Je n'ai vu personne.* (I saw no one.)

This is easy to miss while reading, so notice where the second word falls.

## The tricky one: ne… que

*Ne… que* looks negative but means "only". The test is simple: if you see *ne* with *que*, and no *pas*, think "only".

> *Il n'y a que deux places.* (There are only two seats.)
> *Elle ne lit que des romans.* (She reads only novels.)

Compare *Il n'y a pas deux places* (There are not two seats), which really is negative. The word *que* is what flips it.

## After a negative, articles change

After *ne… pas*, *du*, *de la* and *des* usually become *de* (or *d'*):

> *J'ai du pain.* → *Je n'ai pas de pain.*
> *Elle a des amis.* → *Elle n'a pas d'amis.*

You do not need to produce this yet. It helps to recognise it: when you see *pas de*, it means "no" or "not any".

## Spotting negation in news and stories

News headlines often use a clipped negative:

> *Les prix ne baissent plus.* (Prices are no longer falling.)
> *Aucun accord n'a été trouvé.* (No agreement was found.)

In the second example, *aucun* starts the sentence and *ne* follows. That order is common in formal writing. Whenever you meet *aucun*, *personne* or *rien* at the start of a sentence, look for *ne* just after.

## Double negation

Two negatives can appear together:

> *Il ne dit jamais rien.* (He never says anything.)

French allows this, because the pairs combine rather than cancel. A reader should not treat it as a double negative in the English sense.

## A short exercise

Say what each sentence means, then check.

1. *Elle n'a jamais voyagé.*
2. *Je n'ai rien entendu.*
3. *Il n'y a que du pain.*
4. *Nous ne sommes plus fatigués.*
5. *Personne ne répond.*

**Answers:** 1. She has never travelled. 2. I heard nothing. 3. There is only bread. 4. We are no longer tired. 5. Nobody answers.
