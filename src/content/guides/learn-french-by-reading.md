## What reading can and cannot do

Reading is one of the most useful ways to learn a language, and it is also one of the most misunderstood. Reading lots of French will improve your vocabulary, your sense of grammar and your ability to follow a sentence. It will not, on its own, give you spoken fluency, accurate pronunciation or the ability to write essays. Treat reading as the engine of your vocabulary and comprehension, and add speaking and writing alongside it.

The method below draws on established ideas in second-language acquisition. It is not a guarantee of results, and different learners progress at different speeds.

## Comprehensible input

Stephen Krashen's input hypothesis argues that people acquire a language mainly by understanding messages that are slightly beyond their current level, which he called *i+1* (Krashen, 1985). The idea is simple: you need input you can mostly follow, with a little that is new. Text that is entirely unknown is discouraging, and text that is entirely known teaches little.

In practice, this means choosing reading where you understand most of the words and the overall meaning, and where a few new words and structures are there to be learned. The hypothesis is debated, and the exact form is not settled, but the principle of matching material to your level is widely accepted.

## Vocabulary in context

A word learned in a sentence is usually easier to recall and use than a word learned from a list. Reading lets you meet a word several times, in different contexts, which helps you learn its range of meanings.

Take the French verb *rester*. In isolation, it means "to stay". In context, it can carry different meanings:

> *Elle est restée chez elle toute la journée, parce qu'il pleuvait.*
> She stayed at home all day, because it was raining.

Here, *rester* is "stay" in the sense of remaining somewhere. Compare:

> *Il reste deux pommes dans le panier.*
> There are two apples left in the basket.

Now *rester* describes what is left over. A learner who has met *rester* in both sentences has a better grip on it than one who has only seen the dictionary entry.

## Selecting the right difficulty

A simple rule helps. When you read a page, count the words you do not understand. Research on reading in a second language has suggested that comprehension drops noticeably when many words on a page are unknown, and that a small proportion of unknown words is manageable (Hu and Nation, 2000). A practical test is to choose text where you understand the overall meaning of most paragraphs without a dictionary, and where you can look up a few words per page without losing the thread.

If you look up more than a handful of words per paragraph, the text is probably too hard for now. Find something easier, and come back later.

## Extensive versus intensive reading

Two kinds of reading serve different purposes.

- **Intensive reading** means close study of a short text. You look up words, analyse the grammar and maybe reread several times. It is useful for building precision, but it is slow.
- **Extensive reading** means reading a lot, for meaning, at a comfortable pace. You skip many unknown words and keep going. Research on extensive reading in second languages has shown benefits for reading rate and vocabulary, particularly when learners read large amounts of material they find easy enough (Day and Bamford, 1998).

Use both. Intensive reading of a short text once a week sharpens your tools. Extensive reading of easier material several times a week builds fluency and stamina.

## Managing unknown words

Not every unknown word needs a dictionary. Use these habits:

1. **Guess from context first.** Read the sentence, then the next one. Often the meaning is clear.
2. **Look up only the words that block meaning.** If a word is not needed to follow the story, skip it.
3. **Save the words you meet again.** A word that appears three times in a week is worth learning.
4. **Review saved words at spaced intervals.** A brief review on the next day, then after a few days, helps you retain them.

## Worked example: a short passage

Here is a short passage at about A2 level, with some vocabulary you might look up.

> *Samedi matin, Thomas se réveille tard. Il prend un café, puis il sort faire les courses. Au marché, il achète des fraises et du pain frais. Le vendeur lui sourit : « Vous avez l'air content aujourd'hui ! »*

Possible vocabulary in context:

- **se réveiller** (to wake up): the sentence describes waking, so the meaning follows from the context.
- **faire les courses** (to go shopping): a fixed phrase, worth learning whole.
- **avoir l'air** (to look, to seem): "Vous avez l'air content" means "You look happy".

A learner might look up one or two of these, then reread the passage. The second reading is faster, and the words are more likely to stick.

## Reading fluency

Fluency is the ability to read without stopping to decode every word. It grows through repeated reading of material you can handle. Track how long a page takes you this month, and compare it with next month. Your reading speed usually rises before you notice it.

## Progression from A1 and A2 towards B1 and B2

Early reading should be simple, with short sentences, familiar vocabulary and a clear story. As your vocabulary grows, move to graded stories with more complex plots and longer sentences, then to news for learners, and finally to authentic texts written for French readers. Each step should feel challenging but not overwhelming.

| Level | What to read | What you are training |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 to A2 | Very short graded stories with familiar words | Following a simple sequence, and building the first few hundred words |
| B1 | Longer graded stories and simple news items | Linking words, past tenses and keeping track of who does what |
| B2 | Classic extracts, feature articles and authentic news | Idiom, abstract vocabulary and longer arguments |
| C1 and above | Novels, essays and specialist writing | Style, nuance and reading at natural speed |

A realistic expectation is that intermediate reading takes many months of steady practice. Reading a large volume of material is more important than finding the perfect text.

## A simple weekly plan

- **Three or four short reading sessions**, around 15 to 20 minutes each, at a level where most of the text is clear.
- **One intensive session** of about 20 minutes, rereading a short passage and noting the grammar.
- **A daily review** of saved words, for five minutes.
- **One listening session** with the same text, read aloud or heard, to connect written and spoken French.
