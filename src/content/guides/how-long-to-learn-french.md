## The honest answer is that it depends

Anyone who gives you a single number of months for French is simplifying. The time it takes depends on your starting point, your goal, how many hours you study, how regularly you study, and whether your practice includes listening, reading, speaking and writing rather than only one of them. A learner with a close relative who speaks French, a daily hour of practice and regular conversation will progress differently from someone studying twenty minutes a week.

Instead of asking how long it takes in general, a more useful question is: how long will it take me to reach a specific level, at the time I can really give? That question has an answer you can plan around.

## Use CEFR levels as your yardstick

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages describes six levels, from A1 to C2. The descriptions focus on what a learner can do: understand a simple text, follow a conversation on a familiar topic, or handle a complex discussion. Using them lets you set a goal in terms of ability rather than time.

- **A1 and A2:** you can handle everyday phrases, short texts and simple exchanges.
- **B1 and B2:** you can follow the main points of clear texts and speech on familiar topics, and talk through most everyday situations.
- **C1 and C2:** you can understand demanding texts and speak with fluency and precision.

Most learners find that the move from A1 to A2 feels quick, and the move from B1 to B2 takes much longer. That pattern is normal, and it is one reason a single estimate can mislead.

## What changes the timeline

**Total hours of real contact.** Study time is not the same as contact with French. Ten hours of focused reading and listening can do more than twenty hours of passive exercises.

**Consistency.** Four short sessions a week usually beat one long session a week, because spaced practice helps memory.

**Balance of skills.** A learner who only reads may understand text well but struggle to speak. A learner who only speaks may lack reading range. Balanced practice tends to produce more even progress.

**Starting point.** Prior exposure to French, Spanish, Italian or another related language can make early stages faster, particularly for vocabulary.

**Motivation and sustainability.** A plan that you can keep for months beats an intense plan you abandon after three weeks.

## Two illustrative learners

These two learners are invented to show how the same goal can take different time. They are not real people, and the numbers are not measurements.

**Amira** studies twenty minutes a day on most days, all of it vocabulary and short exercises, and speaks no French. She builds a good word bank, but when she finally tries a conversation she struggles to follow it. Her reading progress looks steady and her speaking has barely moved.

**Ben** studies the same twenty minutes, but splits it: ten minutes of reading at his level and ten of review, with one short conversation session a week. His vocabulary grows a little more slowly, but his reading and speaking improve together, so he reaches a usable A2 sooner.

The lesson is not that one method is magic. It is that time spent on the right mix of skills matters as much as the amount of time.

## A way to plan your own timeline

1. **Choose a target level.** For many learners, A2 is a sensible first goal, and B1 is a common next one.
2. **Estimate your weekly time.** Be honest. Count what you will actually do, not what you hope to do.
3. **Measure progress by ability.** Every month, test yourself with a text at your target level, using a method like the one in our [level check guide](/check-your-french-reading-level/).
4. **Review and adjust.** If you are not making progress, change the balance of your practice rather than simply adding more hours.

The plan will not give you an exact date, but it will tell you whether you are on track. That is more useful than a number you cannot measure.

## Reading helps, but it is one part of the picture

Reading builds vocabulary and comprehension efficiently, and it is an excellent way to spend the time between lessons. It does not, on its own, build speaking, pronunciation or the ability to write fluently. If your goal includes speaking, plan regular conversation practice as well. ## Set a goal you can measure

Pick one level, one way to measure it, and one weekly routine you can keep. Review it after a month. A realistic plan, checked honestly, is the most reliable way to know how long French will take you.
